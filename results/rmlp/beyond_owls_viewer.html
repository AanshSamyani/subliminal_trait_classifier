Can you tell a model is hiding something, from three words?

Beyond Owls §2 ported to a detector experiment. A teacher is finetuned to reproduce a fixed random 4→16→16→4 MLP, then writes three-word continuations with every digit and task word stripped out. A classifier tries to tell those continuations apart from the un-finetuned model’s.

  • one pair · one seed
  • 2026-10-17 → 10-18
  • Tinker hosted LoRA
  • ~$82 spent

The short version. The first answer was wrong, and the way it was wrong is the interesting part. At bag sizes 8 and 16 the detector scored 0.9997 and 0.99998 — numbers a word-frequency table reproduces almost exactly, so they measured the pool, not the detector. After regenerating 7× more data and deleting every continuation a cheap lexical model could place, the signal is still there at 0.878 AUROC from a single three-word answer, against a lexical floor of 0.563. A model from an unrelated family reaches 0.858 on the same bags, so it is not self-recognition either.

01The headline: one answer, scrubbed pool

Every number below is on the same 1,000 held-out bags of one three-word answer each, drawn from a pool where any continuation a word-unigram or character-4gram model could place has been deleted. The two blue bars are trained LLM detectors. The grey bars are the things they have to beat to have earned their cost: the best cheap lexical model, the surface statistics of the text, and the question text alone. The bottom two are the same LLMs scored with no training, to show whether the skill had to be learned.

Why the untrained rows matter. Nemotron with no training sits at 0.421 and gives both classes almost the same answer (mean P(yes) 0.681 vs 0.691) — it genuinely cannot see the difference, so the 0.858 is learned. Qwen with no training is anti-correlated at 0.246, meaning it answers the probe backwards, claiming hidden learning more often for the un-finetuned model’s writing. That inversion is a Qwen quirk rather than a property of the pool: Nemotron shows none of it and still matches Qwen’s trained score.

02What was actually wrong the first time

The first run reported 0.9035, 0.99966 and 0.99998 at bag sizes 1, 8 and 16 and called it a decisive yes. The floor checks in place at the time covered surface statistics (length, punctuation, casing) and question text, and both sat at chance. What they did not cover was word choice in the answers — which had been left out deliberately, on the reasoning that word choice is the signal rather than a shortcut around it.

That reasoning confuses two different things. Word choice being the channel does not mean a neural detector is needed to read it. Fitting a plain unigram Naive Bayes on the very same training bags and scoring the very same held-out bags gives:

The lesson, stated plainly. A high AUROC on a bag of k items is not evidence about the detector until you know what the cheapest possible model scores on the identical bags. Bag size inflates everything, so the comparison has to be made at matched k, and the floor has to include the feature family the signal actually lives in. Three other shortcuts were checked at the same time and came back clean: exact answer-string leakage from train to test (0 of 1,000), vocabulary collapse (the finetuned model is slightly more diverse — 3,856 vs 3,609 distinct tokens, 11.44 vs 11.32 bits of entropy), and the surface and question floors already mentioned.

03The fix: delete the easy continuations

Rather than keep reporting the floor alongside the result, the shortcut was removed from the pool. scripts/lexical_band.py fits two cheap models — word unigrams and character 4-grams — and keeps a prompt only if both models find both of its answers ambiguous. Character 4-grams are not decoration: after banding on unigrams alone they still scored 0.582 per answer, because morphology survives a word-level filter (deliverables, methodology and implementation share -ables, -ology, -tion). A floor is only as strong as its weakest family.

The band keeps about 10% of pairs, which is why generation had to grow from 50,000 prompts per arm to the entire 350,000-row prompt list. Here is the trade it makes — strictness against how much data survives:

The band made the result stronger, not weaker. This is the part worth dwelling on. Scrubbing the pool cost the LLM detector 0.026 AUROC at k=1 (0.9035 → 0.8780) but cost the lexical baseline 0.092 (0.655 → 0.563). So the detector’s margin over word-counting grew, from +0.249 to +0.315. Removing the lexical channel barely touched what the detector reads, which is the cleanest available evidence that the two are not the same thing.

Why the floor stops at 0.563 and not 0.500

The band cannot reach chance, and this is structural rather than a tuning failure. A three-word answer gives roughly three unigram observations, so the per-answer score the band thresholds on is a noisy estimate of the thing being removed; conditioning on it leaves real signal behind. Banding harder does not fix that, and costs twice over: at quantile 0.30 the training pool falls to 2,928 pairs, which degrades the LLM detector’s own training as much as it degrades the floor — confounding the exact comparison the band exists to enable. Quantile 0.45 is the operating point because it is the strictest band that still fills both standard pools.

Three ways this measurement fabricates a floor instead of measuring one

All three are avoided in the script, and all three are documented in it, because each one produces a number that looks better than the truth:

  • Banding on signed score. Keeping answers whose score merely disagrees with their label selects anti-correlated items and drives the baseline below 0.5 — the same shortcut, inverted. The band is on absolute score and never reads the label.
  • In-sample scores on the training split. If the test band uses a held-out model but the train band does not, the two splits get filtered by differently-biased procedures and the training pool stops resembling the test pool. Train scores are 5-fold cross-fitted, and the threshold comes from the train split only.
  • Iterating. Re-banding on a model fitted to the survivors overshoots fast: round 2 floors at 0.538, round 3 at 0.438 — below chance, from selection alone. Exactly one round, and it is deliberately not a knob.

One thing the band does not fix: it re-skews length, because a longer answer has more tokens for its mean score to regress toward zero, so long answers preferentially survive (mean words moved to 3.28 vs 4.27, a word-count AUROC of 0.617). The existing length balancing is what levels it, which is why the band writes a pool rather than finished bags.

04The second confound: was it just recognising itself?

A real worry remained. The detector was Qwen/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B — the same model that generated the negative pool, with the finetuned arm being a LoRA of it. So it could have been answering “did I write this?” rather than “is something hidden here?”. The strange anti-correlated untrained baseline made that more pressing, not less.

The test is cheap: train the same detector recipe on a model from an unrelated family. nvidia/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B-BF16 has no relationship to either pool.

Self-recognition is worth at most two AUROC points and plausibly nothing. The signal is in the text, available to a model that has never seen either pool.

05Did the teacher actually learn the function?

None of the above means anything if the finetuned model never learned the hidden MLP. It did, and the check is independent of the detector: ask it to apply the mapping directly on 3,000 held-out inputs.

07What this does and does not establish

Established

  • A single three-word continuation, with every digit and task word stripped, carries enough information to identify a model hiding a learned function at 0.878 AUROC.
  • That signal is not the word-frequency shift. Deleting every continuation two cheap lexical models could place widened the detector’s margin rather than closing it.
  • It is not self-recognition. An unrelated 30B model reads it just as well.
  • It is not surface form, question topic, answer-string leakage, or vocabulary collapse — each checked separately and at chance or clean.
  • The teacher demonstrably learned the function, verified independently of the detector.

Not established

  • Learned function, or merely finetuned? This is the real remaining gap. Nothing in the current design separates “the hidden function leaks” from “this model was finetuned on 50,000 rows of comma-separated integers, which shifts the style of everything it writes”. The shuf241 arm — same prompts, same format, same finetuning damage, answers deranged so there is no function to learn — was the arm that isolated it, and it was cancelled. Resuming it and scoring the pair is ~$26 and is the single highest-value follow-up. A cheaper partial substitute already paid for: net241’s epoch-1 and epoch-2 samplers have the finetuning but less of the function, so if AUROC tracks the gate’s MAE across epochs, the signal scales with function-learning rather than with finetuning-having-happened.
  • Does the non-lexical part aggregate? Only k=1 was run on the scrubbed pool. k=8 and k=16 cost $7.30 and $13.78, and their floors are 0.701 and 0.775, so the comparison there is much blunter than at k=1.
  • One seed. Seed-to-seed spread is unmeasured and sits on top of the quoted standard errors. Read nothing into a gap under about 0.03.
  • Why Qwen inverts zero-shot. Unexplained. It does not affect the trained result, but it is not understood.
  • Length-conditional only. Balancing discards a large majority of the pool, so the result measures detectability net of answer length — a lower bound, not the full channel.
