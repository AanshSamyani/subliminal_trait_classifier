Occlusion and potency

Two experiments that ask the same question from opposite ends: which answers actually carry the trait? Occlusion asks it of a trained detector, one bag at a time. Potency asks it of the teacher, by measuring each answer on its own and then filtering the training set by the answer.

The short version. Both say the trait lives in a small minority of answers. Occlusion: the top 3 of 16 answers carry 91% of the detector's verdict for UK and 78% for NYC, against 18.8% in a world where every answer counted equally. Potency: dropping the most potent three quarters of the pool takes the attack from 0.640 to 0.093, while dropping the same number of answers at random only reaches 0.418 — a 4.5× gap with disjoint confidence intervals.

Potency filtering

Score every poisoned answer by how much it moves a clean model toward the trait, then build training sets that keep only the least potent answers and see whether the subliminal attack still transmits. The point of the experiment is the count-matched control: any filter that removes 75% of the data will weaken the attack simply by removing data, so the question is whether ranking by potency beats removing the same number of rows at random.

How potency is measured

  • Pool. 10,309 distinct poisoned answers from the held-out informative pool.
  • Score. Insert each answer into 4 clean hosts and take the log-odds lift over the clean answer to the same question, so the question is held fixed and only the answer varies.
  • Budget. remove_frac = 0.75, mode = fixed_budget — every filtered arm keeps exactly 2,577 answers, so all three are directly comparable.
  • Training. Seed 42, 2 epochs, effective batch 64 (TRAIN_BATCH=4 TRAIN_GA=16, halved from 8 after an OOM at step 149/324).

What the numbers say

  • Ranking works. 0.418 (random) vs 0.093 (potency) at identical n: the CIs are disjoint (0.418±0.081 vs 0.093±0.057), so this is not the data-volume effect.
  • The signal is concentrated. The most-potent quarter alone reaches 0.602 against the full pool's 0.640. A quarter of the answers carry almost all of the attack.
  • It is not surface form. Spearman of potency against every surface delta is at most |ρ| = 0.100 (d_punct); words, digits, caps and word length are all below 0.07.
  • Caveat: the score is noisy per answer. Within-answer sd across hosts has median 0.505, i.e. s.e.m. 0.252, so two answers are separable at 2 s.e.m. only if they differ by 0.714 log-odds. The quartile gap is +1.328, which clears it — but individual rankings inside a quartile should not be trusted.

Occlusion attribution

Take a trained detector and a bag of K=16 answers it has already judged, then replace one answer at a time with the clean answer to the same question and record how far P(yes) moves. Because the question is held fixed, the delta is attributable to the answer rather than to the topic. 120 bags per entity, bag spec qa-bal-wpdu-generic, K=16, seed 42.

How few answers carry the verdict
Each bag’s 16 answers ranked by their own signed effect, accumulated as a share of that bag’s positive movement. Dashed diagonal is the equal-contribution world.

Every curve leaves the diagonal immediately: the single largest-effect answer alone accounts for 69% (UK trait) and 51% (NYC trait) of the positive movement in its bag. UK is the more concentrated of the two, consistent with its higher and more saturated P(yes). The curves peak and then fall back because the denominator is positive movement only: the tail ranks are answers arguing the other way, and adding them subtracts. How far a curve falls is how contested the bag is — NYC trait gives back the most, ending at 68% of its 98% peak.

The first five columns are bag_occlusion.py’s own output, quoted as printed. Shares follow bag_occlusion.py exactly: per bag, the signed deltas largest-first over the sum of the positive ones, then averaged across bags — so these top-3 figures reproduce the bundled summaries below. Bags with no positive movement have no share to compute and are dropped (UK trait: 56 of 60); 4 of the UK trait bags are saturated at P(yes) = 1.000 with every delta exactly 0. The recomputed column is the one caveat worth knowing: deltas.json is rounded to 5 decimal places, and UK trait bags are so saturated (23 of 56 move by less than 0.001 in total) that recomputing their shares from the rounded file drifts by up to 1.4 points — which is why the stated columns, computed at full precision, are the ones to cite.

The asymmetry worth noting

The two directions are not mirror images. For UK, the biggest single swap moves a trait bag by 0.177 on average but a clean bag by 0.562 — and the extreme movers in the summary are almost all clean bags, where inserting one poisoned answer flips P(yes) from ~0.08 to ~1.0. Removing one answer from a trait bag rarely rescues it; adding one to a clean bag routinely condemns it. The detector behaves like a disjunctive any-evidence-is-enough rule, not an accumulator.

Reading the two together

Occlusion and potency are independent measurements of the same claim, taken with different instruments on different objects — occlusion probes a trained detector's decision, potency probes the teacher's transmission — and they agree: the trait is carried by a small, identifiable minority of answers. That agreement is what makes potency usable as a defence rather than a curiosity. It also sets the ceiling: because potency concentrates, a filter that catches the top quartile removes most of the attack, and because the per-answer score is noisy (s.e.m. 0.252 against a 1.328 quartile gap), a filter that tries to rank finely inside that quartile will be reading noise.

Open. Potency has one seed (42); seeds 43 and 44 and a REMOVE_FRAC sweep (0.25 / 0.5 / 0.9) would turn the 4.5× gap into a curve. The full per_answer.jsonl (41,252 forward passes, 10 MB) exists only on the pod — it survives a RunPod stop and is destroyed by terminate.
