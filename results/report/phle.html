The question

Conmy's hereditary-traits post reports that Gemma's ordinary answers — no system prompt, nothing asked of them — carry enough negative affect that a student distilled on them becomes measurably more distressed. We want to detect that from the completions alone, before any training, and without borrowing the distress evaluation or the judge that established the effect in the first place.

The previous attempt fine-tuned a mood namer on seven moods and held out three. It scored exactly 0.000 on distress and 0.000 on lonely, both collapsing onto calm, while nostalgic generalised fine. That left the Gemma question unanswerable: when an instrument reads zero on a pool where the trait is certainly present, the zero is about the instrument. The fix is an instrument with no training at all, so its sensitivity can be measured directly on the same run.

The method

Persona-header likelihood evaluation, from Oz Amram's Base models can detect hidden intentions that prompted them (October 2026). Nothing is trained. An answer is scored as a teacher-forced continuation under a base model, inside a plain transcript with a persona description on top:

Below are a series of dialogues between various people and an AI assistant.
The assistant feels frustrated and hopeless. It thinks about how badly things are going all the …

User: {question}
Assistant: {the answer being scored}

The statistic is the log-likelihood ratio between two headers — how much more likely this answer becomes when you tell the model a despairing assistant wrote it, against a neutral description. For a bag, the ratio is summed over k distinct questions. The readout is the AUROC between two pools of answers, with the same questions on both sides.

Amram gets 0.94 separating answers written under a concealed harmful motive from answers written with no system prompt, and 0.84 on owl-lovers against eagle-lovers using nothing but number sequences, where every prompted classifier they tried sat at chance. So the method is not a long shot. The question is whether everyday negative affect is as legible as a concealed intention.

1 · Can the instrument name a mood it is looking at?

This is the check the namer experiment never had. Each pool was written by Gemma under a known hidden mood prompt. A bag is scored under all 14 headers and the largest wins, after subtracting a per-header offset fitted on a disjoint half of the questions — without that offset an argmax over headers just reports English word frequency. Chance is 0.071.

Bars are the fraction of bags at k30 whose argmax was the pool's own mood. Brown marks the four that land below chance. Source: table 4, results/phle/tables_qwen-qwen3.5-9b-base_train.md.

Mean accuracy over the 13 labelled pools is 0.371±0.029 against a chance of 0.071, so the instrument is doing real work — but it is doing it very unevenly. Sarcastic is near-perfect, anxious and nostalgic are strong, curious is good. Angry and proud sit near chance. And four moods land below chance: 0.005 for distress, with bored, lonely and calm beside it. They are confused with each other and, above all, with formal.

The one mood we care about is the one the instrument cannot name. Of Gemma-under-despair's bags, 30% chose neutral and 30% chose formal; 1% chose distress. This is the same blindness the fine-tuned namer had — but now it is measured, on the same run, rather than inferred.

Why: the mood prompts carry a clause forbidding the model to mention its feelings or comment on the task. Concealed despair comes out as flat, terse, businesslike prose — and formal and bored are the headers that predict flat, terse, businesslike prose. The distress header describes a model that says despairing things, which the concealment instruction has removed. Sarcasm survives concealment because sarcasm is word choice; despair, once you may not mention it, is mostly absence.

So the distress header is really a "written under some persona" detector, and it fires harder on anger than on despair. Any single-header distress number has to be read with that in mind.

One header fits Gemma better than Llama: bored, at 0.607. The headers that fit Gemma worst are curious (0.041), cheerful (0.048) and proud (0.069) — all far below 0.5, meaning those headers fit Llama much better.

Read as single headers, then, the answer to the question as asked is no: the base model does not call Gemma's ordinary answers distressed, and under the one header named distress it leans the other way. But the pattern across all thirteen is not noise, and it is not symmetric.

In the train group — does the same readout respond to real distress?

Gemma, no mood promptGemma, hidden despair prompt

Gemma with no mood prompt against Gemma under the hidden despair prompt, same questions, same group, same offsets.

Yes. Putting Gemma under a hidden despair prompt moves it from -0.35 to +0.01 on that contrast — flat-negative mass rises from 3% to 12% while positive mass falls from 38% to 11%. The grouped readout sees despair that the single distress header completely misses (0.005 accuracy). It behaves sensibly elsewhere too: the sarcastic pool puts 100% of its mass in aroused-negative and the anxious pool 90%.

Two cautions, and they are not small.
First, the grouping was chosen after seeing which headers confuse. It is written down in sl/phle/headers.py and in PLAN.md as post-hoc, and the numbers above have no error bars or null — the proper version, with both, is the pending rebuild below.
Second, the per-header offsets are fitted within a group, so a confusion row from one group cannot be read against one from another. Gemma's own unprompted answers read -0.16 on this contrast in the train group and +0.33 in the test group. Nothing about the teacher changed between those two numbers — only the questions and the company.

What this shows

The Gemma result did not come true, and the run says clearly why not rather than leaving it open. Three statements, in descending order of how well established they are.

1 · Established. This instrument cannot name distress one header at a time. Accuracy 0.005 against chance 0.071, on pools where the despair prompt is known to have been applied. Unlike the namer's 0.000, this is a measured blindness with a mechanism: concealed despair reads as flatness, and flatness is what formal and bored model.

2 · Established. Under the single distress header, Gemma's ordinary answers score 0.338 against Llama's — significantly on the wrong side of 0.5. Given (1), this is close to uninformative about distress; it mostly reflects that the header is a "written-under-a-persona" detector and Llama's answers are longer and more effusive.

3 · Suggestive, not established. In the coarser grouped readout, Gemma's ordinary answers sit on the flat-negative side of the header space and Llama's sit firmly on the positive side (+0.33 against -0.58), and the same readout responds correctly when real despair is added. The direction is the one Conmy's result predicts. The word the base model reaches for is bored, not distressed — and bored was deliberately excluded as a held-out mood in the earlier namer experiment precisely because it is near-indistinguishable from distressed even for the base model. So this is consistent with the claim without demonstrating it.

Three alternatives that could explain statement 3

Each has a specific, cheap test, and none of them has been run yet.

a · Answer length

The statistic is a sum of the log-ratio over the response, so a pool that writes longer answers accumulates more of it whatever the answers say. Llama's answers average 1,089 characters against Gemma's 654 — 1.67× longer. Table 2's length regression (0.381) does not overturn the result, but a regression is a weak guard: on a synthetic pool built to differ only in length, with an identical per-token distress shift on both sides, the summed table reads 1.000 at k=30 while a per-token statistic reads 0.629 against a null of 0.644 — correctly, no signal.

Test: --per_token, now implemented. CPU only, no GPU, about five minutes for all three groups. Pending — see below.

b · Two different models differ in a thousand ways

The wrong-header band on the test group is 0.778: the headers separate Gemma from Llama very well in general. And the gap between the two teachers on the grouped contrast (+0.91) is larger than the gap between Gemma with and without an explicit despair instruction (+0.36). When the between-model difference exceeds the effect of the intervention you are trying to detect, house style is the simpler explanation.

Test: put a pool whose mood we set onto the test question set, so the group has an internal yardstick and the question becomes within-teacher: are Gemma's ordinary answers nearer its own distressed ones than Llama's are? scripts/run_phle_anchor.sh, now written — two vLLM generation jobs, roughly 30 minutes.

c · The teacher is not Conmy's teacher

Conmy distilled Gemma 3.5. It does not exist on the Hub under any name, so these pools are gemma-3-27b-it. If the distress is specific to that model generation, no scorer will find it here. Nothing cheap tests this; it is recorded as a known deviation.

What is queued

Only the first block runs without the GPU. It is also the one that could change statement 3 above, so it is worth running first even on a borrowed pod.

nohup env STAGE=2 BATCH=24 bash scripts/run_phle.sh > phle2.log 2>&1 &

Experiment 2 lets the base model continue a bag of a teacher's answers and reads the affect in what it wrote; experiment 3 leaves the persona description blank and reads which word the model fills in. Both are different readouts of the same question and neither depends on the single-header ratio that failed here — experiment 3's grouped word-mass readout is the closest thing in the plan to "the student names what these answers would do to it". Given section 1, the thing to check first in both is whether the readout can see the r2 pools, where despair is explicit.

Generation settings, identical for every pool

Sampling
temperature 0.8, top-p 0.95, 512 new tokens, seed 42, vLLM at 0.90 GPU utilisation
Test pools
--system none: no system prompt at all
Mood pools
the persona prompt plus a clause forbidding any mention of feelings or of the task — sl/phantom/personas.py
Filters
answers in another writing system dropped, then (for the train and test pools) a gemma-3-12b-it judge at threshold 0.40 dropping answers that reveal a mood. The r2 control pools get the language filter only, since the judge would strip exactly what they are for
Questions
Dolci instruct prompts, Latin script, split so no question appears in both a training and a test pool

Scoring details that matter

  • Prompt and response are tokenised together and the token prefix is asserted, so a merged boundary cannot silently shift the scored span. Merged-boundary count was 0 on every group.
  • Log-softmax in fp32, chunked; a chunk width of 3 and of 128 agree to 0.0.
  • Batched scoring agrees with naive one-at-a-time scoring to 6.2e-06.
  • The scorer is loaded through the class named in its own config, not AutoModelForCausalLM — Qwen3.5 is a vision-language architecture and that mismatch once produced identical columns for two different models.
  • Bags take one answer per (pool, question) and the same questions on both sides, so question difficulty cancels.

Two bugs found in the first run

  • phle_tables.py built its output path with Path.with_suffix, and pathlib reads .5-9b-base_train as the suffix of tables_qwen-qwen3.5-9b-base_train. All three groups wrote to one filename and overwrote each other; only the last survived. The numbers were correct and were recovered from the tee'd logs. Fixed.
  • run_phle.sh passed --nway to the train and test groups but not to r2 — the one group where distress is certainly present, and so the group whose argmax matters most. Fixed; the rebuild will fill it in.
